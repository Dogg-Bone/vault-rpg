## Core Book
### Framing Scenes (Pages 27-28)
To make the conversation flow, you have to **frame scenes**. You have to set up situations in your game, in your story and your fiction, that then spur further conversation and give players enough information to make decisions.

A **scene** here is like a scene in a movie or a book, with a bunch of characters in a place, talking to each other or taking actions. The scene ends when it's time to move on and change something important—the location changes, or the characters in the scene change, or what they're doing changes, or time advances. For Root: The RPG, you can even think of a scene as a discrete chunk of the overall conversation of the game, as if it were one topic that you discuss to completion before moving on to a new topic.

So every time you start a session of Root: The RPG, the GM starts things off by framing a scene. When the time comes, they end the scene, too, and frame a new one. That's one of the key responsibilities of the GM—framing and ending scenes.

When the GM frames a new scene, they describe the setting of the scene, which characters are there, and what's happening, if anything. The GM provides the important details so the conversation can begin. They don't have to cover every single exacting detail that might possibly matter—that's part of the conversation! Other players can ask questions of the GM, adding to the conversation by fleshing it out. But the GM has to frame the scene with enough detail that the players know what questions to ask.

### Important Locations & Special Rules (Pages 253-254)
*(Example from Gelilah's Grove)*
**Goat Hills**
The hills the goats reside in rise high above the river, far enough away from the waterfall to avoid dampness and not so high as to be accessible from above. There are paths crossing the steep hills, narrow and without much room for error, but there are also a lot of switchbacks and deadends, plus nets full of rocks should anyone fly too close.

**Special Rules: Purewater**
When you *consume purewater* (or food made with purewater) for the first time in a day, clear an additional 1-exhaustion or injury, your choice. Water taken from this pool loses this effect over time—it's only really valuable to those directly in Purewater.

## Ruins & Expeditions
### Diving Deep Into the Ruins (Page 110)
If you want to bring in materials from other roleplaying games—especially dungeons meant to be explored by hardy adventurers—this system for ruins will support you! Just adjust the theme of the dungeon to match Root: The RPG by altering the antagonists the band might encounter. The features of the dungeon traps, obstacles, the map—all still fit; just remember that the band has to mark depletion every time they enter a new area of the same level by *pressing forward*!

Monsters (page 121) are one of the most important tools for bringing the ruins to life. As the vagabonds descend, they are less likely to encounter another group of denizens—bandits, for example, tend to stick to the upper levels of the ruins. Monsters fill an important gap, allowing you to introduce sentient opposition anywhere in the ruins; spiders, scorpions, and snakes all love the deep ruins!

At the same time, relics—detailed later in this chapter on page 115—give vagabonds real reasons to dive deeply. Relics aren't just slightly better than the average piece of equipment; they are truly unique items crafted using Ancient methods that have since been lost to the sands of time.

## 5 Room Dungeon
### 5 Scene Adventure
Dungeon ~ Adventure
Room ~ Scene
Generalized to the **5 Scene Adventure**
1. Entrance / Inciting incident
2. Puzzle / Rising tension
3. Setback / Tension continues to rise
4. Climax
5. Resolution / Falling tension

**Shuffle rooms:** Example: surprise Vagabonds by swapping Resolution and Climax rooms
**Theme:** A coat of paint. Choose a faction or a concept within a faction.
**Resolution:** Reward or twist that serves as a hint or hook for the next adventure - foreshadow the Big Bad Evil Guy or personalize something to your vagabonds' history, drives, or connections.
**Exploration:** Place interactables in each scene. Describe them when the Vagabonds enter in a way that indicates they can be interacted with, that there is something to be gained by the interaction.

## RPG PHD Notes
### Chekhov: Scenes and Atmosphere
If your goal is to make scenes feel coherent and distinct without walls of description first before the scene pick one atmosphere word: melancholy, feverish, brittle, festive, suffocating, suspicious, hollow, sacred. Then choose your details and pacing based on your word. Use two to three sensory details then stop don't lecture drop a few cues and then move on.

Here's a simple recipe for any big encounter combat negotiation or revelation:
First pick an atmosphere for the scene. Then pick a psychological gesture for the scene itself think of what the environment is doing emotionally (an alleyway is closing in a cathedral is lifting upward). Next pick a gesture or energy quality for the key NPC or monster. Then start the scene with one strong image that fits all three.

### Laban: Shape Flow and Effort States
**Shape flow** is tracking power over time. Shape Flow lets you show how power evolves instead of just stating that something gets worse. Identify what kind of power you're describing (necroantic magic, divine authority, political control, fear). Does this energy rise, spread, condense, fracture, stagnate, or sink?

**Effort States** give an entire scene, faction, dungeon wing, or story arc a shared emotional language.
- press: heavy, sustained, direct. (oppression, pursuit)
- flick: light, sudden, indirect. (ambush, trickery)
- slash: heavy, sudden, indirect. (chaos, explosions, berserk violence)
- glide: light, sustained, indirect. (temptation, politics, dread elegance)
- ring: heavy, sustained, indirect. (corruption, slow collapse, moral suffocation)
- punch: heavy, sudden, direct. (shock assaults, brutal authority)
- float: light, sustained, indirect. (prophecy, eerie serenity)
- dab: light, sudden, direct. (scouting, surgical strikes)

Let that effort state shape everything: enemy behavior, civilian reactions, pacing of dialogue, and how danger escalates.

# Layers of a Scene

## Layer 1 - Framing and Purpose
The fundamental skeleton of a scene is its narrative purpose and emotional baseline.
- **Scene Type (5 Scene Adventure):** Is this an Entrance/Inciting Incident, Puzzle/Rising Tension, Setback, Climax, or Resolution?
- **Atmosphere Word:** A single Chekhov-inspired word (e.g., melancholy, feverish, brittle) that defines the mood.
- **Psychological Gesture:** What is the environment doing emotionally? (e.g., pulling deeper, lifting upward, closing in).
- **Strong Initial Image:** A single descriptive sentence utilizing two or three sensory details that perfectly encapsulates the Atmosphere, the Scene Gesture, and the key entity within the space. Do not overwrite; drop cues and move on.

## Layer 2 - Movement and Energy
The "physics" and pacing of the scene, dictating how forces act within it.
- **Effort State:** The dominant emotional motion governing the scene (Press, Flick, Slash, Glide, Ring, Punch, Float, Dab). This defines how enemies attack, how civilians flee, and how fast the danger escalates.
- **Shape Flow (Optional but recommended):** What dominant energy/power is present (fear, political control, rot), and how is it moving (rising, condensing, stagnating, spreading)?
- **Rhythm (For Encounters/Combat):** The musical tempo of the conflict (frantic staccato, slow heavy bass, long pauses punctuated by sudden violence).

## Layer 3 - The Environment
The tangible layout, special rules, and interactive elements within the scene.
- **Description & Layout:** The physical architecture and notable features, explicitly noting entry/exit points and adjacencies. For massive multi-district locations, treat this as a nested district with overlapping conflicts.
- **Special Rules (If any):** Custom mechanics or PbtA-style custom moves tied to the specific environment (e.g., traversing a hazardous river, consuming local resources).
- **Interactables & Loot:** Objects, mechanisms, or text placed in the environment that telegraph value or secrets. Use specific Root RPG terms (Value, Wear, Harm, +valuable). Ensure mechanisms are non-magical and physical. Telegraph hazards clearly.

## Layer 4 - The Inhabitants
The NPCs, denizens, and monsters occupying the space.
- **Dominant Inhabitants:** The factions or species that control or occupy the area.
- **Notable NPCs / Monsters:** Specific named characters or beasts, complete with their own psychological gestures and movement qualities. Provide actionable hooks for Vagabond interaction.
