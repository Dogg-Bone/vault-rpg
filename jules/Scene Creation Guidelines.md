# Core Book

## Framing Scenes
As the GM in Root: The RPG, your primary way of interacting with the players is by framing scenes and managing the spotlight. Framing a scene means setting the stage: describing where the vagabonds are, who is there with them, what is happening around them, and most importantly, what immediate pressures they face.

Always end your scene framing or your reaction to a player's action with the fundamental question: **"What do you do?"** This passes the spotlight to the players, prompting them to act and driving the narrative forward. When a player triggers a move, resolve it, and then frame the resulting situation before asking the question again.

## Creating Clearings
When creating a clearing, you are establishing the overarching location where many scenes will take place. Every clearing requires:
- **Control:** Which faction currently holds power here.
- **Dominant Inhabitants:** The primary species or groups living in the clearing.
- **Important Locations:** Key areas where scenes can unfold, such as markets, strongholds, or ancient trees. Include architectural details and atmosphere.
- **Notable NPCs:** The prominent figures driving the clearing's narrative.
- **Problems/Conflicts:** At least two distinct issues currently plaguing the clearing.
- **First Session Encounters (2d6):** A table of encounters specifically designed to tie into and reflect the clearing's established problems, helping kick off gameplay.

# Travelers and Outsiders Book

## Expanded Locations & Fractal Clearings
For larger, more complex clearings, you can utilize the "fractal clearing" concept. This involves breaking a massive, multi-district clearing down into smaller, interconnected sub-districts.

When detailing a fractal clearing, you must generate full clearing details (control, conflicts, NPCs, custom moves, 2d6 encounter tables, and rumors) for both the overarching clearing and each individual nested district. Ensure that their details and conflicts intersect logically, creating a rich tapestry of localized scenes that feed into the larger narrative.

# Ruins and Expeditions Book

## Designing Ruins and Hazards
Ruins are specialized locations that require a focus on exploration, danger, and history. When creating scenes within ruins or designing hazards:
- **Telegraph the Danger:** Clearly describe the warning signs of hazards (e.g., crumbling stone, strange smells, unnatural silence) to enable informed decision-making before the players blunder into them.
- **PbtA-style Complications:** Do not provide direct, singular solutions to hazards. Instead, present the obstacle and let the players devise a solution. Detail the potential PbtA-style complications that could arise on a 7-9 or a 6-.
- **Layout and Navigation:** Include specific building layout details (entry/exit points, adjacencies) to make the physical space matter in the scene.

# RPG PHD Notes

## Atmosphere and Psychological Gestures
To make scenes feel coherent and distinct without walls of description, start by picking one **Atmosphere** word (e.g., melancholy, feverish, brittle, festive, suffocating). Choose your sensory details and pacing based on this word. Drop a few targeted sensory cues and move on.

Next, pick a **Psychological Gesture** for the scene itself—what the environment is doing emotionally.
- *Examples:* An alleyway is *closing in*, a cathedral is *lifting upward*, a forbidden archive is *pulling deeper*.
Combine the atmosphere, the scene gesture, and the prominent NPC's personal gesture to create one strong opening image.

## Rhythm and Phrasing
Decide the core **Rhythm** of a scene or combat. Think in musical terms rather than mechanical ones.
- *Examples:* Rapid percussion, slow bass, a rising crescendo, or long unsettling pauses.
Design encounters by rhythm first, selecting creatures, terrain, and tactics that support that tempo, changing how time feels at the table.

## Shape Flow
Track power over time using **Shape Flow** to show how a situation evolves rather than just stating that things get worse. Identify the kind of power present (e.g., divine authority, political control, fear) and decide how its energy moves over time.
- *Examples:* Does the energy *rise, spread, condense, fracture, stagnate,* or *sink*?
Describe the environment reacting to that flow before describing the effect itself.

## Effort States
Give an entire scene, faction, or dungeon wing a shared emotional language by assigning a dominant **Effort State** (Weight, Time, Space, Flow).
- **Press:** Heavy, sustained, direct (oppression, siege).
- **Flick:** Light, sudden, indirect (ambush, trickery).
- **Slash:** Heavy, sudden, indirect (chaos, berserk violence).
- **Glide:** Light, sustained, indirect (temptation, politics).
- **Wring:** Heavy, sustained, indirect (corruption, psychological torment).
- **Punch:** Heavy, sudden, direct (shock assaults, executions).
- **Float:** Light, sustained, indirect (prophecy, wandering ghosts).
- **Dab:** Light, sudden, direct (scouting, surgical strikes).
Let this effort state shape everything: civilian reactions, terrain usage, dialogue pacing, and danger escalation.

# 5 Room Dungeon

## The 5-Scene Adventure
The 5 Room Dungeon format can be generalized into a **5-Scene Adventure**, treating it as a 5-part narrative challenge rather than a literal dungeon.
1. **Entrance / Inciting Incident:** The threshold of the adventure.
2. **Puzzle / Rising Tension:** An obstacle requiring thought, negotiation, or skill rather than pure combat.
3. **Setback / Tension Continues to Rise:** A complication, trap, or a shift in the stakes.
4. **Climax:** The primary conflict or confrontation.
5. **Resolution / Falling Tension:** The aftermath, reward, or a twist that serves as a hook for the next adventure.

Shuffle these scenes (e.g., surprise Vagabonds by swapping Resolution and Climax) to keep the narrative fresh.

## Exploration and Interactables
Place interactables in each scene. Describe them when the Vagabonds enter in a way that indicates they can be interacted with. These can be documents, objects, environment mechanisms, or specific NPCs for social interactions (trickery/intimidation). Provide clues rather than solutions.

# Layers of a Scene

## Layer 1 - Framing & Structure (The Skeleton)
This layer defines the mechanical and narrative purpose of the scene. Where are we, what is the structure of the adventure, and what are the immediate, actionable details?
- **Location:** The physical space (Clearing, District, Ruin) and its architectural layout/adjacencies.
- **Narrative Role:** The scene's place in the 5-Scene framework (Entrance, Puzzle, Setback, Climax, Resolution).
- **Conflict Tie-in:** Which of the location's overarching problems/conflicts this scene actively pushes forward.
- **Telegraphing & Hazards:** The immediate, actionable warning signs of danger or complications present in the space.

## Layer 2 - Atmosphere & Flow (The Flesh)
This layer governs the sensory experience, pacing, and emotional weight of the scene. How does the environment feel, and how does the energy move?
- **Atmosphere Word:** A single, anchoring emotional descriptor (e.g., suffocating, feverish, brittle).
- **Sensory Cues:** Two to three specific sensory details (sight, sound, smell) that establish the atmosphere without over-explaining.
- **Scene Gesture:** The emotional direction of the environment (e.g., pulling deeper, closing in, lifting upward).
- **Shape Flow:** The type of power present (e.g., fear, authority, magic) and how it moves (e.g., condensing, spreading, stagnating).
- **Rhythm:** The pacing of the scene (e.g., frantic staccato, slow deliberate bass, sudden pauses).
- **Effort State:** The dominant motion quality governing the scene's entities and environment (Press, Flick, Slash, Glide, Wring, Punch, Float, Dab).

## Layer 3 - Interaction & Escalation (The Action)
This layer covers what the vagabonds can actually *do*, who is there, and how the GM drives the scene forward.
- **Notable NPCs:** Who is present, what is their drive, and how does their personal psychological gesture interact with the scene's gesture?
- **Interactables & Loot:** Specific documents, mechanisms, or oddities that reward exploration. Ensure loot uses mechanical terminology (Harm, Wear, Value).
- **PbtA Complications:** The specific ways things can go wrong on a 7-9 or 6- roll. How do the hazards or NPCs react when the players fail?
- **The Call to Action:** The immediate pressure presented to the players, ending with the prompt: *"What do you do?"*

## Core Book
### Framing Scenes (Pages 27-28)
To make the conversation flow, you have to **frame scenes**. You have to set up situations in your game, in your story and your fiction, that then spur further conversation and give players enough information to make decisions.

A **scene** here is like a scene in a movie or a book, with a bunch of characters in a place, talking to each other or taking actions. The scene ends when it's time to move on and change something important—the location changes, or the characters in the scene change, or what they're doing changes, or time advances. For Root: The RPG, you can even think of a scene as a discrete chunk of the overall conversation of the game, as if it were one topic that you discuss to completion before moving on to a new topic.

So every time you start a session of Root: The RPG, the GM starts things off by framing a scene. When the time comes, they end the scene, too, and frame a new one. That's one of the key responsibilities of the GM—framing and ending scenes.

When the GM frames a new scene, they describe the setting of the scene, which characters are there, and what's happening, if anything. The GM provides the important details so the conversation can begin. They don't have to cover every single exacting detail that might possibly matter—that's part of the conversation! Other players can ask questions of the GM, adding to the conversation by fleshing it out. But the GM has to frame the scene with enough detail that the players know what questions to ask.

### Important Locations & Special Rules (Pages 253-254)
*(Example from Gelilah's Grove)*
**Goat Hills**
The hills the goats reside in rise high above the river, far enough away from the waterfall to avoid dampness and not so high as to be accessible from above. There are paths crossing the steep hills, narrow and without much room for error, but there are also a lot of switchbacks and deadends, plus nets full of rocks should anyone fly too close.

**Special Rules: Purewater**
When you *consume purewater* (or food made with purewater) for the first time in a day, clear an additional 1-exhaustion or injury, your choice. Water taken from this pool loses this effect over time—it's only really valuable to those directly in Purewater.

## Ruins & Expeditions
### Diving Deep Into the Ruins (Page 110)
If you want to bring in materials from other roleplaying games—especially dungeons meant to be explored by hardy adventurers—this system for ruins will support you! Just adjust the theme of the dungeon to match Root: The RPG by altering the antagonists the band might encounter. The features of the dungeon traps, obstacles, the map—all still fit; just remember that the band has to mark depletion every time they enter a new area of the same level by *pressing forward*!

Monsters (page 121) are one of the most important tools for bringing the ruins to life. As the vagabonds descend, they are less likely to encounter another group of denizens—bandits, for example, tend to stick to the upper levels of the ruins. Monsters fill an important gap, allowing you to introduce sentient opposition anywhere in the ruins; spiders, scorpions, and snakes all love the deep ruins!

At the same time, relics—detailed later in this chapter on page 115—give vagabonds real reasons to dive deeply. Relics aren't just slightly better than the average piece of equipment; they are truly unique items crafted using Ancient methods that have since been lost to the sands of time.

# Layers of a Scene

## Layer 1 - Framing and Purpose
The fundamental skeleton of a scene is its narrative purpose and emotional baseline.
- **Scene Type (5 Scene Adventure):** Is this an Entrance/Inciting Incident, Puzzle/Rising Tension, Setback, Climax, or Resolution?
- **Atmosphere Word:** A single Chekhov-inspired word (e.g., melancholy, feverish, brittle) that defines the mood.
- **Psychological Gesture:** What is the environment doing emotionally? (e.g., pulling deeper, lifting upward, closing in).
- **Strong Initial Image:** A single descriptive sentence utilizing two or three sensory details that perfectly encapsulates the Atmosphere, the Scene Gesture, and the key entity within the space. Do not overwrite; drop cues and move on.

## Layer 2 - Movement and Energy
The "physics" and pacing of the scene, dictating how forces act within it.
- **Effort State:** The dominant emotional motion governing the scene (Press, Flick, Slash, Glide, Ring, Punch, Float, Dab). This defines how enemies attack, how civilians flee, and how fast the danger escalates.
- **Shape Flow (Optional but recommended):** What dominant energy/power is present (fear, political control, rot), and how is it moving (rising, condensing, stagnating, spreading)?
- **Rhythm (For Encounters/Combat):** The musical tempo of the conflict (frantic staccato, slow heavy bass, long pauses punctuated by sudden violence).

## Layer 3 - The Environment
The tangible layout, special rules, and interactive elements within the scene.
- **Description & Layout:** The physical architecture and notable features, explicitly noting entry/exit points and adjacencies. For massive multi-district locations, treat this as a nested district with overlapping conflicts.
- **Special Rules (If any):** Custom mechanics or PbtA-style custom moves tied to the specific environment (e.g., traversing a hazardous river, consuming local resources).
- **Interactables & Loot:** Objects, mechanisms, or text placed in the environment that telegraph value or secrets. Use specific Root RPG terms (Value, Wear, Harm, +valuable). Ensure mechanisms are non-magical and physical. Telegraph hazards clearly.

## Layer 4 - The Inhabitants
The NPCs, denizens, and monsters occupying the space.
- **Dominant Inhabitants:** The factions or species that control or occupy the area.
- **Notable NPCs / Monsters:** Specific named characters or beasts, complete with their own psychological gestures and movement qualities. Provide actionable hooks for Vagabond interaction.
