## Core Book
### The Woodland Explored (Pages 13-24)
The Woodland itself is ancient, and denizens of myriad kinds have been carving out lives there for ages. Some denizens even tell stories today of ancient empires or cultures that once spread out where the trees now grow—all that is left of them are ruins grown over with roots and ivy.
Everything changed some time ago when the denizens began to work together, creating clearings within the Woodland where they could live together in safety. They cut back the forest and turned some trees into buildings. They tamed the growth until there was a boundary past which the Woodland could not encroach.
In particular, there is one act that sets all vagabonds apart from the rest of the Woodland: vagabonds are willing to travel through the forests. More impressive still, vagabonds are capable of accomplishing this feat with a fair chance of surviving. Most other denizens know better; they stick to the paths or clearings, the safe spaces, the places where authorities and guards are around to deal with bandits or bears or other threats. But the vagabonds, they'll cut through deep, thick forests, potentially traveling faster than any path-bound force. If the pay is right, they'll gladly troop into forests to root out bandits, or find their way to ancient ruins in search of relics and treasure.

## Ruins & Expeditions
### The Ancients (Pages 99-100)
Vagabonds have always had a special relationship with the ruins of the Woodland. For many denizens, even the ruins nearest to their clearings are sources of danger, filled with monsters and bandits that emerge only to cause trouble. Vagabonds, however, see the potential the ruins have to offer—shelter, food, gold, maybe even precious relics of a time long ago.
Yet, just as surely as the Ancients once ruled all they touched...they disappeared. Today, the uppermost shoots of their underground kingdom have become the ruins of the Woodland, filled with their treasures—both literal and cultural—and relics, the remaining artifacts and objets d'art that represent the vast and amazing technology the Ancients regarded as commonplace. Nothing else remains.
It is not clear if the Ancients fled the Woodland, were wiped out by the darkness, or merely dissolved as a culture...but they are no longer present in the Woodland. Their mark, however, is eternal, both in the ruins they left behind and in the myths and legends told about them today.

## Travelers and Outsiders
### Using Locations in Play (Page 134)
After your players have made their characters and connected them to each other, take a break. While your players have a snack or use the restroom, look at your preparation for your starting place, be it a clearing or a ruin. If any changes occur to you to hook into the PCs and their issues, ways to introduce NPCs who matter to them, or objects they care about, make those changes. When your players come back from their break, make sure you speak a bit about why the PCs are going to this location—looking for treasure in a ruin, hoping to repair equipment at a clearing, etc.

## High-Yield Location Prep (Lazy DM Concepts)
- **Evocative Labels:** Instead of mapping every square inch, define distinct areas within the location and give them evocative, inspiring labels (e.g., "Radiant Sinkhole" rather than just "sinkhole", "Moldering Archive" rather than "library").
- **Notable Features:** Focus on 3 notable features per room or distinct area that the characters can see, interact with, or use to their advantage.
- **Actionable Secrets & Clues:** Have a list of secrets or pieces of lore that can be dropped into the location wherever it makes the most sense during play.
- **Dynamic Situations, Not Static Plots:** Outline the current situation (who is doing what and why) and the impending escalation.

## RPG PHD Notes
### Atmosphere & Shape Flow
- **Chekhov & Scenes:** Pick one atmosphere word (e.g., melancholy, feverish, suffocating). Choose details based on your word. Use two to three sensory details, drop a few cues, and then move on.
- **Laban & Shape Flow:** Track power over time. Identify the kind of power (authority, fear, influence) and how it moves (does it rise, spread, condense, fracture, stagnate, or sink?).
- **Effort States:** Give a location or faction a shared emotional language (Press, Flick, Slash, Glide, Wring, Punch, Float, Dab). Let that effort state shape everything: enemy behavior, civilian reactions, and the pacing of dialogue.

## Moldy (Stratified) Worldbuilding
### Concepts
"Moldy worldbuilding" (also referred to as 'stratified worldbuilding' or 'palimpsest worldbuilding') refers to environments where the current state of the world is built upon the remnants of the past, characterized by multiple layers or strata of rebuild, growth, evolution, and decay cycles.

# Layers of a Location

When designing a point of interest for Root: The RPG—whether it's a ruin, a prominent building, a natural landmark, or a plaza—treat it as a static but evocative stage. Unlike clearings (which are macro-political entities) or scenes (which are time-bound narrative moments), a location is a persistent place waiting to be explored.

## Layer 1 - Foundation & Geography (The Bones)
This layer defines the physical reality of the location. Where is it, what does it look like, and what secrets are buried within its architecture?
- **Atmosphere & Effort State:** Pick one core atmosphere word and a guiding Effort State (e.g., Wring, Float, Press) that dictates the general rhythm and emotional feel of the location.
- **Shape Flow:** Track what kind of dominant power or energy exists in the location (e.g., lingering death, stagnant authority, creeping nature) and how it moves (spreading, condensing, sinking).
- **Moldy / Stratified History:** What was this place originally? How is the current location built upon or deteriorating the ruins, infrastructure, or remnants of previous eras?

## Layer 2 - Layout & Evocative Details (The Flesh)
This layer breaks the location down into manageable, interactive spaces using high-yield prep principles.
- **General Description:** 2-3 paragraphs describing the immediate sensory details and architecture visible upon arrival.
- **Distinct Areas (Evocative Labels):** Divide the location into 3-5 distinct areas. Give each an evocative name (e.g., "The Flooded Undercroft" instead of "Basement").
- **Notable Features:** For each distinct area, list 2-3 notable features. These should be things the characters can interact with, use as cover, investigate, or steal. Ensure they fit the non-magical woodland aesthetic.

## Layer 3 - Encounters & Interaction (The Nerves)
This layer covers the actionable elements for the players. Who or what is currently occupying the space, and what can the vagabonds discover?
- **Current Occupants / Hazards:** Who is here right now, and what are they doing? Or, what passive hazards make the space dangerous?
- **Rumors, Secrets & Clues:** Prepare a list of actionable secrets or clues that can be organically discovered while exploring the location.
- **Loot & Resources:** Specific items, documents, or valuables hidden within the location. Use Root RPG mechanical terminology (Harm, Wear, Value, +valuable tags, etc.).
