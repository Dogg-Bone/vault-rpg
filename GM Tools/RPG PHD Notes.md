# Chekhov Psychological Gestures
Anchoring the NPC with a thought.
What you do need is a way to anchor your instincts so NPCs don't all sound the same and scenes don't wander and your improvisation has shape instead of being static.
They're fast internal tools that let you give characters and scenes emotional clarity in just a few seconds and you don't necessarily perform them unless you want you just think them and then your delivery shifts on its own.
## Technique 1: Want becomes psychological gesture
If your goal is to make an NPC feel consistent and motivated then first decide what this NPC wants in the scene. Keep it super simple. Do they want to:
- push the PCs away 
- win them over 
- control them 
- cling to them 
- lift them up
- etc.
Some simple psychological gestures might be:
- pushing away 
- pulling toward 
- grabbing or clutching 
- reaching out 
- lifting up 
- shielding or protecting 
Next, pick a gesture that matches that want. Again, you never have to perform it unless you want to you. Just imagine it for a second or two.
#### Example
Your NPC is an old general who wants the party to toughen up and do the hard thing, so the gesture might be pushing forward as if shoving someone into battle. You do the gesture in your mind before you speak. Literally take a breath and in your head you shove something forward, then talk. Let it color your delivery and don't overthink it. You might find yourself naturally using firmer phrasing, shorter sentences, or a more insistent tone: *"Enough waiting. We've seen what you're doing out there. Every hour you sit here more villages burn. Get up, take your steel, and do something!"*

The same NPC with a different gesture, say "clutching". This would be someone who's afraid to lose them: *"Listen I've - I've buried too many kids in armor, don't make me add your names to the list. If you're going you go smart, you hear me?"* 
Same character sheet, completely different vibe.

## Technique 2: Center
If your goal is to make each NPC feel physically different, first pick where their center lives.
- Head: this might be if they're mental, curious, anxious, calculating
- Chest: if they're proud, heroic, emotional, dramatic
- Hips: sly, playful, grounded
- Stomach: might be instinctive, predatory, stubborn
Now imagine them talking from that place:
- Head voice: up, lots of "um, actually" energy
- Chest: open, declarative, confident
- Hips: relaxed, sideways comments, teasing
- Stomach: low, slow, deliberate, "I'm not moving for you" kind of energy
Next, apply it in one line of dialogue. 

For example, a head-centered scholar might say, *"If we cross reference the sigils on the obelisk with the old guild records we might oh that's interesting look at that crack over there."* 

Chest centered paladin: *"We stand or fall together. I'll take the front. If anything breaks us, it goes through me first."* 

A stomach centered orc chieftain: "You come into my valley, you talk or you bleed. I do not care which." 

You don't have to describe any of this, although you certainly can. Just feeling the center of the character can help change how you speak. In other words, these traits can give you a way into character that's different than how you might have approached them in the past. 

## Technique 3: Verb
If you're working on a villain, decide what this villain does to people emotionally. Do they crush hope? Do they invade boundaries? Do they tempt? Do they smother? Do they devour?

Next, turn that into a gesture or atmosphere. So a crusher gesture might be crushing downward. A manipulator gesture is reaching in.

A devourer *atmosphere* might be smoldering hunger, a cold mastermind atmosphere might be glacial stillness. Let that drive every interaction. 

A crushing tyrant, for example: "Your stories bore me," he says, leaning back on the throne. "Kneel, or I'll show you how I make problems smaller." Short, downward language: "bore," "kneel," "smaller." 

Or maybe they are a reaching manipulator: "You look exhausted. Sit, have some wine, tell me what he promised you and we'll see if I can't sweeten the deal." Everything is an invitation that feels like a hand inside your personal space.

Or a smoldering demon: It looks at you the way a starving man looks at bread he's not yet allowed to touch. Even when it smiles its jaw never quite closes. 

And then you can resummon them later with the same impulse next session. All you need to remember is this guy crushes, or this woman reaches, or this thing hungers, that's it. You're not doing Chekhov in any formal way, you're just stealing some very smart shortcuts to make your instinct sharper and your table's experience clearer.

# Laban Movement Theory

### Effort States
**Press:** Strong, sustained, direct
**Wring:** Strong, sustained, indirect
**Slash:** Strong, sudden, indirect
**Punch:** Strong, sudden, direct
**Float:** Light, sustained, indirect
**Glide:** Light, sustained, direct
**Flick:** Light, sudden, indirect
**Dab:** Light, sudden, direct
## Movement Quality
### Step one
before the session, tag your major enemies with four qualities.
Weight, Time, Space, and Flow
**Weight:** Light or Heavy
**Time:** Sudden or Sustained
**Space:** Direct or Indirect
**Flow:** Free energy or Bound energy

You can write something like: 
- ogre - heavy, sustained, direct, bound 
- wraith assassin - light, sudden, indirect, free 
### Step two
when combat starts, let those qualities drive your first description. you don't describe attacks yet, you describe how the fight begins. the ogre doesn't roll initiative, it causes the floorboards to complain as it steps forward and refuses to be rushed. the wraith doesn't take its turn, it erupts from a blind angle that shouldn't exist.
### Step three
let the qualities guide your improvisation when the rules go blurry. if you're unsure whether an enemy would press or reposition, its qualities answer that for you. direct presses, indirect repositions, sudden spikes, sustained grinds. 
### Step four
when the fight turns, let the same motion language dictate collapse. a free flow creature dies in wild momentum, a bound one locks, cracks, and finally comes apart in something like structural failure. so once you start using this, you're not asking what does it do. this round, you're asking how does its nature continue to express itself under pressure.
## Kinespheres
kinespheres let you design presence before action. it determines how close danger feels before anything actually happens.

first, decide how far this creature's influence extends, not mechanically, but emotionally. a small kinesphere means danger feels localized, a medium kinesphere means danger fills the room, a massive kinesphere means danger exists before the creature is even visible.

next, show the kinesphere before the creature. you always describe effects first, not the enemy itself. dust shifting, heat building, sound changing, NPCs reacting, light behaving strangely. the environment signals the scale of danger before the stat block ever arrives. 

also, let social kinesphere override distance. a noble can dominate a hall with posture alone. a demon can make a cathedral feel claustrophobic. a lich can make even distance feel unsafe.
## Inner attitude vs outer motion
this lets you telegraph lies, pressure, and danger without saying it outright.

first, decide what the character truly feels or intends, their inner attitude. before you think about how the NPC speaks or acts, decide what's actually happening inside them. this is the emotional or psychological truth of the moment. are they afraid? wanting to dominate? are they desperate, protective, manipulative, loyal, guilty, calm? you only need one clear internal driver. for example, a noble is terrified of losing political power. a merchant secretly wants to trap the party in a debt. a warrior sincerely wants peace but doesn't trust anyone. 

next, decide how their energy actually moves. this is their outer motion. choose how that inner attitude moves through space, time, and force. is it pressing forward, withdrawing, gliding, smoothly flicking around, condensing, spreading, hesitating? this is not about personality, it's about direction and pressure.

here's some example pairings:
- fear with a withdrawing motion
- control with a pressing motion
- guilt with a condescending motion

next, let the mismatch or alignment generate the tension. if inner attitude and outer motion align, the scene feels straightforward. if they clash, tension sparks instantly. 
for example, 
- aligned: a nervous scholar who has ***fear*** and ***withdrawing***. maybe they speak softly, they keep backing away, they avoid commitment.
- if it's mismatched: a friendly merchant who has a ***predatory intent*** plus a ***pressing*** motion. he smiles warmly while tightening conversational space. the words feel safe, the pressure does not.

next, express motion through space and pacing, not explanation. you never explain the mismatch, you let it leak through rhythm, through distance, and through structure. do they interrupt? do they allow silence? do they take space, or retreat from it? do options feel open, or narrowing? 

example: a charming cult leader speaks softly and kindly, but every response closes another exit, removes another option, and reenters the conversation on dependence. the players feel trapped without a single hostile word being spoken. 

also, you can apply this to entire factions, cities, or institutions. this isn't just for NPCs, it scales up. a city that claims to welcome outsiders but whose social motion presses conformity. a religious order that preaches humility while its influence steadily spreads upward into law and politics. 

players begin to sense when a system's self-image doesn't match its real behavior.
## Movement as narrative identity

this is world building through motion.

first, choose a dominant movement logic for a group, culture, or power. instead of starting with ideology, alignment, or politics, start with motion. ask one simple question: how does this group move through the world over time? do they advance steadily, drift indirectly, burst violently, condense slowly?

for example, an authoritarian empire might be pressing, sustained, direct. a fey court might be light, indirect, floating. a religious crusade might be rising, spreading force. a secret cabal could be circling, withdrawing, compressing. once chosen, this movement logic becomes the group's emotional fingerprint. 

now, apply that motion to everything they do before you ever explain what they believe. how they patrol, how they threaten, how they negotiate, how they celebrate, how they retreat. a pressing culture, for example, doesn't negotiate in circles, they push. a floating culture avoids confrontation entirely. a slashing culture escalates without warning. players understand who these people are before anyone explains a doctrine. these scenes of consistent motion can do more than paragraphs of lore. 

here's what I love most about Laban: none of this requires more prep. you don't need new stat systems. you don't need complicated choreography. you need a few words like heavy, sudden, gliding, pressing, and suddenly your descriptions wake up, combat starts feeling alive instead of procedural. monsters stop feeling interchangeable. your world gains a physical fingerprint players can recognize instinctively.

## Effort States
effort states are a way that you give an entire scene, faction, dungeon wing, or story arc a shared emotional language. instead of deciding every creature individually, you decide the dominant emotional motion of the situation.
### step one 
pick one dominant effort state for the scene or faction. choose the emotional engine that best fits what the scene feels like. 
so:
- press: heavy, sustained, direct. for a siege and interrogation, oppression and pursuit. 
- flick: light, sudden, indirect. for ambush, trickery, swarms and fey mischief.
- slash: heavy, sudden, indirect. for chaos, explosions, and berserk violence.
- glide: light, sustained, indirect. for temptation, politics, illusions and dread elegance.
- ring: heavy, sustained, indirect. for corruption, psychological torment, slow collapse, cursed endurance and moral suffocation.
- punch: heavy, sudden, direct. for shock assaults, executions, duels, brutal authority, decisive violence and rule by force.
- float: light, sustained, indirect. for prophecy, dreams, spirits, wandering ghosts, altered states and eerie serenity.
- dab: light, sudden, direct. for scouting, interrupts, trap triggers, surgical strikes, and quick thinking under pressure.
### step two
let that effort state shape everything. once chosen, it governs enemy behavior, civilian reactions, how the train gets used, the pacing of dialogue and how danger escalates. the next thing is to keep it consistent. as long as the scene persists, maintain the same movement logic. the players may never notice the label you choose, but they will absolutely feel that the world is behaving with emotional consistency
